How the model works and where it is weak
  • Weather. Hourly Open-Meteo data (NOAA HRRR and GFS) on a 0.6° grid of 154 points from 36.5 to 42.5°N and 96.5 to 88.7°W: wind at 10, 80, 120 and 180 m and at 950, 925, 900 and 850 hPa (placed at their true height above ground using geopotential height and terrain), boundary layer height, precipitation, shortwave radiation and cloud cover. It is fetched fresh on every load and every 30 minutes after. Nothing is cached, and if a refresh fails for 90 minutes the page blanks the result.
  • Particles. 12,000 model particles in eight size bins from 0.3 to 80 µm, with mass split by a lognormal distribution (geometric standard deviation 2.5). They start spread evenly between the ground and the cloud top and move in 50 second steps with wind interpolated in space, height and time.
  • Settling. Stokes fall speed with the Cunningham slip correction and a Schiller-Naumann drag correction for the larger sizes. For lead oxide that is about 0.3 mm/s at 1 µm and 3 cm/s at 10 µm.
  • Turbulent spread. A Pasquill class (A to F) is set each hour at each grid point from 10 m wind, sunlight and cloud cover (Turner's method). The class sets turbulence intensity. Horizontal spread is a correlated random walk with a 1,000 s memory. Vertical mixing is a random walk that reflects at the ground and at the boundary layer top. When the boundary layer drops at night, particles above it stay aloft in weak turbulence.
  • Dry deposition. Particles that settle to the ground deposit all their mass. Particles in the lowest 30 m also lose mass at a deposition velocity from a resistance model (aerodynamic plus surface-layer resistance, with Brownian diffusion and impaction, friction velocity from the 10 m wind over 0.1 m roughness).
  • Wet deposition. In rain, each particle loses mass at 8.4×10⁻⁵ × (mm/h)^0.79 per second.
  • Source. Nobody has published how much lead left the site. By default the release is set so the modeled deposition matches the two contour areas on the Army's pre-detonation map, and the default cloud top (100 m) and particle size (25 µm median) are the pair that reproduced both areas at once on the Nov 25 weather. Other combinations can fit nearly as well. That ties this model to the Army's model. It says nothing about what was measured.
  • Checks. Modeled mass is conserved to rounding error. The fitted release changes by under 10% with a different random seed, four times the particles or half the time step. The modeled track (southwest, about 135 km out after five hours) lines up with the time markers on the Army dispersion map.
  • Readouts. "Peak air" is the average over a 20 km radius and the lowest 100 m. "On ground" is the average over the same radius. Both are smoothed and will miss local hot spots. The Army's dispersion map draws its air contour at 0.18 µg/m³, the detection limit of its air sampling method.
  • Weak points. Hours after the fetch time are forecast. The weather grid is about 50 by 65 km, so terrain, river valleys and local wind shifts are missing. There is no chemistry, no resuspension of deposited dust, and no blast or buoyancy model for the first minutes. The Army's maps come from DTRA modeling with information this page does not have. Where the two disagree, trust the Army's maps, and trust measurements over both. Do not use these numbers for health decisions.

About this simulator

Open source. The code, tests and method notes are on GitHub.

It runs a particle dispersion model in your browser using live Open-Meteo weather data, and sets its release amount so the result lines up with the Army's published deposition map. It also pulls the Army's latest maps so you can compare them side by side.

It is an independent estimate with no connection to the U.S. Army, EPA or any agency. It measures nothing and can be wrong. For measured lead levels and instructions use the EPA and Army links on the page.
