How the model works and where it is weak
  • Weather. Hourly Open-Meteo data. For the first day that is NOAA's 3 km HRRR model (checked by requesting it by name and comparing), then GFS. A main grid of 154 points at 0.6° covers 36.5 to 42.5°N and 96.5 to 88.7°W: wind at 10, 80, 120 and 180 m and at 950, 925, 900 and 850 hPa (placed at their true height above ground using geopotential height and terrain), boundary layer height, precipitation, shortwave radiation and cloud cover. A wide grid of 168 points at 1.5°, from 27 to 43.5°N and 98.5 to 79°W, carries fewer levels and is used once dust leaves the main grid. Between levels the wind is interpolated in the logarithm of height, as in a logarithmic wind profile, and below 10 m it follows the log law for 0.1 m roughness. Data is fetched on every load and every 30 minutes after, through a shared copy that is at most 10 minutes old, and the page shows the fetch time. If refreshing fails for 90 minutes the page hides the result.
  • Particles. 48,000 model particles in ten size bins (a quick 12,000 particle pass shows first) from 0.2 to 80 µm physical diameter, with mass split by a lognormal distribution (geometric standard deviation 2.5). They start spread evenly between the ground and the cloud top, with turbulent velocities drawn from the surrounding air, and move in 50 second steps with wind interpolated in space, height and time. Air monitors and the terms PM10 and PM2.5 use aerodynamic diameter, which is the physical diameter times the square root of the density relative to water. Lead oxide is 9.5 times denser than water, so PM10 means about 3 µm and under here, and PM2.5 about 0.8 µm and under.
  • Settling. Stokes fall speed with the Cunningham slip correction (Seinfeld and Pandis 2016) and a Schiller-Naumann drag correction for the larger sizes. Air viscosity (Sutherland's law), density and mean free path are computed from the forecast temperature and pressure at the site. For lead oxide that gives about 0.3 mm/s at 1 µm and 3 cm/s at 10 µm, so PM2.5-size lead stays airborne for days while 10 µm particles are gone within about a day.
  • Turbulent spread. A Pasquill class (A to F) is set each hour at each grid point. By day it comes from 10 m wind and solar radiation (the EPA solar radiation method, EPA-454/R-99-005 Table 6-7). At night it comes from 10 m wind and cloud cover (Turner's key in the same report). Turner's overcast rule needs a cloud ceiling, which is not available, so an overcast night is treated as cloudy. The class gives turbulence intensity (Gifford 1976), which is multiplied by the 10 m wind. For class D that works out to about 2.1 and 1.0 times the friction velocity, close to the ratios usually quoted for a neutral surface layer. Horizontal spread is a correlated random walk whose memory time (330 s) is set so the crosswind width follows Draxler's 1976 curve between one and three hours. Vertical mixing is a random walk that reflects at the ground and at the boundary layer top. When the boundary layer drops at night, particles above it stay aloft in weak turbulence.
  • Dry deposition. Particles that settle to the ground deposit all their mass. Particles in the lowest 30 m also lose mass at a deposition velocity from a resistance model (Slinn 1982, as given by Seinfeld and Pandis 2016: aerodynamic plus surface-layer resistance, with Brownian diffusion and impaction, friction velocity from the 10 m wind over 0.1 m roughness).
  • Wet deposition. In rain, each particle loses mass at 8.4×10⁻⁵ × (mm/h)^0.79 per second (Baklanov and Sørensen 2001). That rate was derived for particles under 2.8 µm and is applied here to every size, so washout of coarse dust is understated.
  • Measurements. EPA runs four real-time lead monitors along the plume path, 6 to 43 km southwest of the site, and publishes their readings. The page reads them live. The model tracks what each monitor would see (fine particles, 10 µm and under, near the ground within 1.5 km, in 15 minute averages) and compares the totals.
  • Source. Nobody has published how much lead left the site, so the release is bounded from data, two ways. The default is a limit from the EPA monitors: the dose they recorded above their pre-detonation average, plus two standard errors of the background noise, divided by the dose the model puts at the same monitors per kilogram released. The exact detonation point is not public and the plant is several km across, so the calculation is repeated with the point moved 3 km in four directions, and the page uses the largest result (never more than the Army case). The other way is the Army pre-detonation map: the release at which the modeled deposition covers the same areas as its two contours. That map is a planning forecast and is the high case. The cloud top (300 m) and particle sizes (30 µm median) are assumptions, chosen because they come closest to reproducing both Army contours.
  • Checks. Automated tests confirm that mass is conserved, that settling speeds match hand calculations, that a dust layer mixes to an even profile and stays even, that airborne mass decays at the rate the settling and deposition speeds imply, and that crosswind spread follows Draxler's curve within 20%. On the Nov 25 weather the modeled track (southwest, about 135 km out after five hours) lines up with the time markers on the Army dispersion map.
  • Readouts. A town counts as reached once one ten-thousandth of the released PM10-size dust is airborne within 20 km of it, at any height. "Peak air" is the average over a 20 km radius and the lowest 100 m. "On ground" is the average over the same radius. Both are smoothed and will miss local hot spots. The Army's dispersion map draws its air contour at 0.18 µg/m³, the detection limit of its air sampling method.
  • Wind check. The page compares the model's 10 m wind with National Weather Service observations at eight airports for the hours already past. A one-off check against the Davenport weather balloon at 6:00 AM CST on Nov 25 found the model within 8° in direction and about 2 m/s in speed at 925 and 850 hPa.
  • Sensitivity. Halving and doubling the constants that have no published source (vertical mixing time, turbulence above the boundary layer, horizontal memory time) moved arrival times by 15 minutes or less and the monitor limit by about 15% on the Nov 25 weather.
  • Snow and rain. Precipitation washes dust out using one rate fitted for rain and for particles under 2.8 µm. Snow scavenges differently and the model does not treat it separately.
  • Weak points. Hours after the fetch time are forecast. The main weather grid is about 50 by 65 km and the wide one about 150 km, so terrain, river valleys, fronts and local wind shifts are smoothed. One forecast is used, with no ensemble, so the far end of the track is the least certain. Vertical air motion is ignored. There is no chemistry, no resuspension of deposited dust, and no blast or buoyancy model for the first minutes. The Army's maps come from DTRA modeling with information this page does not have. Where the two disagree, trust the Army's maps, and trust measurements over both. Do not use these numbers for health decisions.

About this simulator

Open source. The code, tests and method notes are on GitHub.

It runs a particle dispersion model in your browser using live Open-Meteo weather data, and sets its release amount so the result lines up with the Army's published deposition map. It also pulls the Army's latest maps so you can compare them side by side.

It is an independent estimate with no connection to the U.S. Army, EPA or any agency. It measures nothing and can be wrong. For measured lead levels and instructions use the EPA and Army links on the page.
